# Food Forest Info Hub — Content Plan
*First deliverable of the [[Hawaii Food Forest Venture]] project. Compiled 2026-08-22.*

## Purpose & positioning
A free, practical information site on planning and building food forests — the first thing the venture ships, ahead of any product sales. Two audiences, one core promise: **you can take real ownership of a piece of your food supply chain, and here's exactly how.**

- **Homeowners** — "I want to grow real food on my own land instead of just landscaping it."
- **Restaurants** — "I want ingredients I can trust, grown by people I actually know, instead of an anonymous distributor line."

The site earns trust with real information given away free, then becomes the natural place both audiences land when they're ready to go further (buy from the South Kona venture, hire design help, source starts/plants, etc.). No hard sell up front — the free content *is* the pitch.

## Content pillars & article outlines

Each pillar below is one hub article. Write these first — everything else in the site links back to them.

### 1. What Is a Food Forest?
- Definition: a self-sustaining, multi-layer planted ecosystem grown for food, modeled on how a natural forest actually organizes itself, rather than a row-crop garden
- Why it's different from a garden or an orchard (perennial, layered, low-input once established)
- What it can realistically produce, and on what timeline (see Pillar 6)
- Who it's for: backyard homeowner vs. small commercial grower — same design logic, different scale

### 2. Planning Your Site
- Observe before you plant: at least one season watching sun path, water pooling, wind — don't skip this
- Permaculture zone thinking: put what you'll tend and harvest most often closest to where you actually walk each day
- Map the site (hand sketch or a free tool like Google Earth) and note existing trees, slope, access
- Site assessment checklist (this becomes a downloadable template — see Resources)

### 3. Design: The Layers & Guilds
- The seven-layer model, explained plainly, top to bottom:
  1. Canopy (tall fruit/nut trees)
  2. Understory (smaller/dwarf fruit trees)
  3. Shrub (berries, coffee, etc.)
  4. Herbaceous (perennial vegetables, herbs)
  5. Groundcover (living mulch, low spreaders)
  6. Root (underground crops — kalo fits here)
  7. Vine (climbers using the vertical space)
- What a "guild" is: a small cluster of plants placed together because they help each other (nitrogen fixers, pest deterrents, pollinator draws)
- Worked example guild using real Hawaiʻi species (see Pillar 4)

### 4. Plant Selection (by climate)
- Launch scope: Hawaiʻi / wet-tropical first, since that's real, lived experience — expand to other climates only once there's real content to back it, not speculative advice
- Species list grounded in what's actually proven on the South Kona site: kalo, ʻōlena, koa, plus common companion species for each layer
- Native vs. introduced food species — when each makes sense
- A short "invasive species to clear first, not plant" list (Guinea Grass, Maile Pilau, Cat's Claw, Christmas Berry — all real, already-cleared examples from the South Kona site)

### 5. Soil, Water & Establishment
- Building soil before/while planting (the site's own vetiver-hedge erosion control is a real example to use)
- Water: catchment, mulching, drip vs. none once established
- Erosion control basics for sloped tropical sites

### 6. Timeline & What to Expect
- Year 1: shrub and herbaceous layers already harvestable
- Years 3–7: canopy trees reach production
- Decade+: the system becomes genuinely self-regulating
- Set expectations honestly — this is the single most useful thing a free info site can do that a nursery upsell won't

### 7. Maintenance & Harvest
- What "low maintenance once established" actually means in practice (it's not zero)
- Pruning, chop-and-drop mulching, succession planting as things die back or get harvested
- Realistic harvest rhythms — what's available and when

### 8. Glossary
Short, plain-language definitions for every term used above (guild, canopy layer, chop-and-drop, permaculture zone, understory, groundcover, nitrogen fixer, polyculture, food forest vs. forest garden). Every other article links terms here on first use.

## Audience-specific sections

### For Restaurants — Own Your Ingredients
- Why a direct grower relationship beats an anonymous distributor line: freshness, traceability, a real story to put on the menu
- What building that relationship actually takes (this is the honest version, not marketing): lead time, volume consistency, seasonal gaps, pricing that works for both sides
- The venture's own labor-equity angle, explained plainly — growers share in the value of what they grow, which restaurants can point to as part of their own sourcing story
- CTA: join the interest list / reach out about the South Kona venture once that's ready to supply

### For Homeowners — Start Your Food Forest
- Backyard-scale version of the planning pillar — smaller footprint, same logic
- Common beginner mistakes (planting canopy trees too close together, skipping the observation season, ignoring water)
- CTA: downloadable site-assessment worksheet, plus the venture's own future offering (consulting/design help, plant starts) once that's real

### Case Study: Nā Hoa Ululāʻau (South Kona)
One case study, used in both audience tracks, told honestly at its current real scale — this is the credibility anchor for the whole site:
- Real, already-documented results: invasive species named and cleared, kalo now producing, ʻōlena yielded 130 lbs of raw root this season, koa replanted, vetiver hedge for erosion control
- What's still in progress — don't overstate; being honest about early-stage status builds more trust than polish
- The direct line from this restoration work to the commercial venture, and to this website

**Further Reading** — real, credible external resources found in research, to link out to rather than duplicate:
- [Permaculture Apprentice — How to Create a Food Forest](https://permacultureapprentice.com/creating-a-food-forest-step-by-step-guide/) — solid step-by-step reference, useful to compare our own outline against
- [Agroforestry.org](https://agroforestry.org/) — nonprofit, publishes *The Overstory* (free agroforestry journal); good long-form reference source
- [Portland Food Forest Initiative](https://pdxfoodforest.org/) — nonprofit doing homeowner lawn-to-food-forest conversion; a close analogue worth studying for site structure and messaging
- [Project Food Forest](https://projectfoodforest.org/) — another nonprofit model, public food forests + classes
- [Denver Urban Gardens — Food Forests](https://dug.org/gardens-food-forests/food-forests/) — community-orchard model, useful for the "public good" framing
- On the restaurant side: [OpenTable — Farm-to-table sourcing guide](https://www.opentable.com/restaurant-solutions/resources/restaurant-guide-to-sustainable-sourcing/) and [WebstaurantStore — Local Food Sourcing Guide](https://www.webstaurantstore.com/article/1105/local-food-sourcing.html) — both good models for how to talk to a restaurant audience specifically

*(Worth an explicit look at Portland Food Forest Initiative and Project Food Forest as direct structural comparables before finalizing the site map above — both are nonprofits doing homeowner-facing food forest education already, closest existing analogues found.)*
