# Reading Grant Deadlines Correctly

If you're building a food forest or a land-restoration project in Hawaiʻi, real
funding exists for it — but a posted deadline on Grants.gov often isn't the
whole story. This is a short list of real programs relevant to that kind of
work, with the specific thing to check on each one before you assume it fits
or trust the date shown.

## Why this exists

A grant program's Grants.gov listing shows a submission deadline. It doesn't
always show whether that deadline applies to you specifically, whether the
program even accepts a direct application from an organization like yours, or
whether this year's actual funding priority still matches the program's name.
Finding that out took a real, avoidable near-miss: almost writing a full
application against a posted deadline that turned out to be for applicants
already invited into the process — the real cutoff for a first-time contact
had already passed, because the program requires reaching out to program
staff before any application exists at all.

That mistake is common enough, and costly enough in wasted effort, that it's
worth writing down plainly rather than keeping it to ourselves.

## What to check on any program, not just these

- **Who can actually apply.** Some programs fund a state agency directly, and
  a nonprofit or small organization can only participate as a named partner
  on that agency's own application — not as a solo applicant.
- **Whether the process is contact-first.** Some programs require reaching
  out to program staff before an application exists at all — the posted
  "deadline" may only apply to applicants already in that conversation.
- **Whether this year's specific funding priority still matches the
  program's name.** A program can keep the same name and general authorizing
  purpose year to year while its actual funding priority shifts.
- **Whether a shared program number actually means the same program.**
  Federal assistance-listing numbers are sometimes shared by more than one
  real, separately-run funding notice — read the specific one you're looking
  at.

## Programs worth knowing about

### Partners for Fish and Wildlife Program (USFWS)
*Federal*

**Why it's relevant:** Funds invasive-species control, native out-planting, and habitat restoration on private land — close to the core work of building a food forest that includes real ecological restoration, not just planting.

**How it actually works:** Contact-first, not a cold written submission. The program's own terms require reaching out to local Partners for Fish and Wildlife staff before an application exists — a biologist works with you to shape the project first.

**The deadline lesson:** A Grants.gov posting can show a submission deadline that only applies to applicants already invited into the process. There can be an earlier, separate cutoff for a first-time contact — always ask directly rather than trusting the posted date alone.

**Where to start:** Pacific Islands Fish and Wildlife Office — pifwo_admin@fws.gov, (808) 792-9400

### Hoʻōla ʻĀina (Green Fee), DLNR / Division of Forestry & Wildlife
*State (Hawaiʻi)*

**Why it's relevant:** State-funded, specifically for community-based restoration of forests, watersheds, and wetlands — as close a mission match to food-forest and land-restoration work as any program tracked here.

**How it actually works:** A full proposal (not just a letter of intent) submitted through the state's HANDS portal during an open RFP window — the window is not year-round.

**The deadline lesson:** This program isn't listed on Grants.gov at all — it's state, not federal — so a federal-only search will miss it entirely. Worth checking DLNR's own announcements directly, not just Grants.gov.

**Where to start:** dlnr.hawaii.gov/blog and hands.ehawaii.gov — watch for the next RFP announcement

### USDA Urban & Community Forestry Challenge Cost Share Grant Program
*Federal*

**Why it's relevant:** Sounds like a food-forest match by name alone — a real caution, not a real fit every year.

**How it actually works:** Each annual cycle has its own stated funding priority, which can shift year to year. One recent cycle's actual focus was wood utilization (turning pruning and removal waste into lumber, biochar, and similar products) rather than ecosystem restoration or food-bearing tree planting.

**The deadline lesson:** A program's title can describe its general authorizing purpose without describing what a specific year's funding call actually prioritizes. Read the current year's own funding priority before assuming a promising-sounding name is a fit.

**Where to start:** grants.gov — search the program name for the current cycle's specific funding priority

### Community Forest and Open Space Conservation Program (USDA Forest Service)
*Federal*

**Why it's relevant:** Relevant if the real need is acquiring new land to protect, not restoring land already held.

**How it actually works:** Funds fee-simple acquisition of forest land by qualified nonprofits, tribes, or local governments. Applications route through the state's own State Forester, not directly to the federal agency.

**The deadline lesson:** Two different real programs can share the same federal assistance-listing number (CFDA) — one national, general-purpose version, and a separate, geographically-restricted version funded under a different initiative. Read which specific funding notice you're looking at, not just the number.

**Where to start:** Hawaiʻi Division of Forestry & Wildlife (state forester's office)

### Competitive State Wildlife Grant Program
*Federal, administered through the state*

**Why it's relevant:** Funds native-species habitat work, but the money typically flows to the state wildlife agency first.

**How it actually works:** Usually the state agency (in Hawaiʻi, DLNR) is the direct applicant; a community organization would most likely need to be a named project partner or subawardee under the state's own application, not a solo direct applicant.

**The deadline lesson:** A program being 'open' on Grants.gov doesn't mean a small organization can apply to it directly — check who the actual eligible applicant is before assuming a posted deadline applies to you.

**Where to start:** Hawaiʻi DLNR — ask directly whether community partners can be named on the state's application
