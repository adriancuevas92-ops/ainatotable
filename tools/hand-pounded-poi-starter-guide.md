# Hand-Pounded Poi: A Starter Checklist

A practical companion to [How Poi Is Made](../articles/how-poi-is-made.md). If you're already growing kalo, hand-pounded poi is one of the most direct ways to turn a harvest into something you can share or sell — Hawaiʻi's food code treats it differently from every other processed food, and the real requirements are short enough to fit on one page.

---

## What the law actually requires

Hawaiʻi's food safety code exempts poi made *only* by hand — taro pulverized with a stone implement on a wooden board — from the certified food-processing establishment and permit that other producers (milled poi included) need. Three conditions still apply:

- [ ] **Food safety certification** — a short online course, generally under $15
- [ ] **A handwashing station**, used during preparation — most working farms already have one
- [ ] **Standard food labeling** on whatever you package and sell

That's the whole list. No commercial kitchen, no building permit, no industrial equipment.

## What it actually costs to get started

| Item | Realistic range |
|---|---|
| Food safety certification | Under $15 |
| Handwashing station | Often $0 — already exists on a working farm |
| Pōhaku kuʻi ʻai (stone) + papa kuʻi ʻai (board) | Low hundreds of dollars |
| Labeling/packaging | Minor, standard cost |

For most kalo growers, the real cost of getting started is closer to a few hundred dollars than anything requiring outside financing.

## If you plan to sell to a restaurant, resort, or retailer

Selling directly to an individual or at a farmers market is covered by the three conditions above. Selling into an institutional kitchen or a retail shelf usually comes with its own additional expectations — most commonly a food-safety (GAP) audit and proof of liability insurance — separate from the state's hand-pounded exemption itself. Hawaiʻi has offered a no-cost GAP audit option for farmers in the past; check current availability with the Hawaiʻi Department of Agriculture before assuming a cost either way.

## If your kalo is upland (dryland), not wetland

Traditional poi is made from wetland (loʻi) taro. Upland taro — increasingly common on Hawaiʻi Island — generally produces a lower-viscosity poi with different souring characteristics. This doesn't rule anything out; it's worth knowing before your first batch, and worth tasting for yourself rather than assuming either way.

## If you're a nonprofit

Selling a food product commercially can raise unrelated business income questions for a 501(c)(3), separate from anything above. If that applies to you, talk to a nonprofit tax advisor before selling at real volume — this is genuinely worth getting right early rather than after the fact.

## Where this fits

Hawaiʻi Island hasn't had a commercial-scale poi mill since 2017 (see [How Poi Is Made](../articles/how-poi-is-made.md) for that story) — small, direct, hand-pounded producers are effectively the whole local supply right now. If you're already growing kalo, this is one of the lowest-barrier ways to do something real with it.
