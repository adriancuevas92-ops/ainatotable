# Direct-to-Consumer Markets

The academic record settles the growing question: this land, and this district, can produce real volume. Māla Kaluʻulu is proving it a few miles away right now. What's less settled — and what this page lays out plainly, with real sources — is the demand side: who actually buys this, and where the real opportunity sits. The honest answer isn't "there's no market." It's that the market is real, underdeveloped, and blocked by access more than by anything else.

## The gap is access, not interest

For breadfruit specifically, the clearest evidence comes directly from the people already selling it:

- **"The demand by far outstrips the supply at the moment,"** according to the Hawaiʻi ʻUlu Cooperative itself, cited directly in a 2022 peer-reviewed study of Hawaiʻi's breadfruit farmers.
- The same study found farmers working through a cooperative sell 52% of what they harvest on average — farmers going it alone sell only 28%. Statewide, an estimated 27% of harvested breadfruit goes to waste.
- Asked which sales outlet paid the most per fruit, the largest group of surveyed farmers (42%) said selling locally and directly — not farmers markets, not a web shop, not any other channel.

That's a real, farmer-reported pattern: the crop sells for more when it's sold directly, and most of what's failing to reach a buyer is failing because of market access, not because nobody wants it.

**The scale still unrecovered is real too.** The historical kaluʻulu belt is estimated at roughly 100,000 breadfruit trees in the Kona region alone. Hawaiʻi's total today, across the whole state, is around 8,000. There's real room to grow into here — this isn't a market that needs to be invented.

## Where kalo actually sells today

Kalo tells a more mature-market story, and it's worth being precise rather than dramatic about it:

- **Upland taro — the style grown on this site — sells for $2.19 per pound**, the highest of any taro product category in Hawaiʻi, well above wetland (loʻi) taro's $1.24 and lūʻau leaf's $1.31 (Hawaiʻi Department of Agriculture, 2021 survey).
- **Retail and restaurant sales make up just 7.3% of the entire statewide taro market by volume.** The industry runs mostly on two much larger channels: poi processors (about half of all volume) and wholesalers (about 38%). Direct, local, restaurant-facing sales are a small, underbuilt slice of an otherwise healthy industry.
- Most taro trade is inter-island bulk — largely Kauaʻi's wetland taro moving to Oʻahu's poi processors — which accounts for 63% of all sales. Home-island, direct sales are the minority pattern, not the industry's main business.

Worth stating plainly: the state's own 2021 report explicitly pushes back on claims that taro imports are about to exceed local production — that specific fear isn't supported by the data. The honest opportunity here isn't a supply crisis. It's a real, underdeveloped, higher-value channel sitting mostly unused inside an industry that's built around a different kind of buyer.

## The same pattern, close to home

Every figure above points to the same shape of problem: not a lack of demand, and not a lack of capacity, but a lack of direct paths between the two. Direct, local sales are the channel breadfruit farmers themselves report as most profitable. Upland kalo's highest price sits specifically in the smallest, most direct channel. A direct grower relationship — the kind described on [For Restaurants](restaurants-own-your-ingredients.md) — is one way that gap actually closes.
