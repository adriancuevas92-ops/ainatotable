# Soil, Water & Establishment

Design and species selection get the attention, but what actually determines whether a food forest survives its first two years is what's happening at ground level: soil, water, and erosion control. This is the least glamorous part of the process and the most decisive one.

## Build soil before — and while — you plant

You rarely get to wait until soil is "ready" before planting; in practice, soil-building and planting happen together. A few approaches that work well in a wet-tropical setting:

- **Chop-and-drop mulching.** Instead of removing cut vegetation from the site, cut it and drop it right where it fell (or move it to where you want mulch). It breaks down in place, feeding the soil and suppressing weeds without adding fertilizer inputs.
- **Nitrogen-fixing companions.** Trees and shrubs like koa (in the canopy) or pinto peanut (as groundcover) build soil fertility as part of the design itself, rather than requiring you to feed it separately.
- **Living cover over bare ground.** Any period where soil sits bare is a period where it's eroding or losing structure in a wet climate. Groundcover species go in early, not last.

## Water: catchment, mulch, and knowing when to stop irrigating

In a wet-tropical climate, the water question is often less about supplying it and more about managing it well:

- **Mulch holds moisture** at the root zone and reduces how much supplemental water young plants need.
- **Catchment** — directing roof or hillside runoff toward planted areas rather than letting it run off the site — can cover a meaningful share of a food forest's water needs without any irrigation infrastructure at all.
- **Drip irrigation**, where used, is usually a first-year-or-two tool to get young plants established, not a permanent requirement. A mature, well-mulched food forest in a climate like Hawaiʻi's wetter regions often needs little to no irrigation once established.

## Erosion control on sloped sites

If your site has any real slope — common in Hawaiʻi's upland agricultural land — erosion control isn't a nice-to-have, it's a prerequisite. Bare, sloped soil in a heavy-rain climate can lose more topsoil in one storm than years of careful soil-building can replace.

A vetiver hedge — a dense, deep-rooted grass planted in contour lines across a slope — is a real, low-cost erosion-control method already in active use on the South Kona reference site this content draws from. It's not decorative: the root system physically holds the slope while the food forest above it establishes.

## What "establishment" actually means

Establishment is the period between planting and the point where a food forest is genuinely holding its own — roots deep enough to find water without help, canopy dense enough to shade out weed competition, soil healthy enough to support the system without constant input. That period is real work: watering (if needed), weeding around young plants before they can out-compete on their own, and replacing anything that doesn't survive. It typically runs one to three years depending on species and site. What happens after establishment is covered next, in [Timeline & What to Expect](06-timeline-and-what-to-expect.md).
