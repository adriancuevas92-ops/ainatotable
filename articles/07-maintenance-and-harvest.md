# Maintenance & Harvest

"Low maintenance once established" is true, and it's also the most misunderstood claim about food forests. It doesn't mean no maintenance — it means the *kind* of work shifts, from establishment labor (weeding, watering, replacing losses) to a smaller, steadier set of tasks that keep a mature system healthy and productive.

## What ongoing maintenance actually looks like

- **Pruning.** Canopy and understory trees need occasional pruning — for shape, for light access to the layers below them, and to manage size on a site with limited room. This is planned work, done a few times a year, not constant attention.
- **Chop-and-drop mulching.** The same technique used during establishment (see [Soil, Water & Establishment](05-soil-water-and-establishment.md)) continues indefinitely: cut back fast-growing groundcover or nitrogen-fixing shrubs periodically and let the cuttings feed the soil in place.
- **Succession planting.** Nothing in a food forest lives forever. As individual plants age out, get harvested to the point of needing replacement, or simply die, something goes back into that spot — ideally something that fills the same guild role. This is where you're actively managing the system rather than just watching it, even at maturity.
- **Pest and disease watching, not spraying.** A well-designed guild is already doing a lot of pest management through diversity and pollinator draw. The maintenance task here is mostly observation — catching a problem early — rather than routine treatment.

## What a realistic harvest rhythm looks like

A food forest doesn't hand you one big harvest event. It gives you a rolling one:

- **Daily-to-weekly:** herbs, leafy greens, some root crops as you need them
- **Seasonal:** most fruit, according to each species' own season — which is part of why a diverse planting matters, since it spreads your harvest window across the year instead of concentrating it
- **Occasional/large:** root crops like kalo, harvested in batches rather than continuously; nut trees, once mature, often produce in a concentrated season

For a household eating directly from the system, this rhythm is a feature — there's usually *something* ready. For a commercial operation supplying a farmers market stand or a restaurant relationship, this same rhythm is a planning problem worth solving deliberately: matching what's actually in season to what you've committed to deliver, and being honest with a buyer about what won't be available year-round from a single planting. (See [For Restaurants — Own Your Ingredients](restaurants-own-your-ingredients.md) for how that conversation actually goes.)

## The maintenance curve over time

Maintenance is heaviest in the first one to three years (establishment), drops significantly once the canopy closes and the layers start supporting each other (roughly years 3–7), and settles into its lowest, steadiest state at full maturity (a decade or more in). See [Timeline & What to Expect](06-timeline-and-what-to-expect.md) for the fuller picture of how that maturation actually unfolds.
