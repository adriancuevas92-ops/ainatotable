# For Homeowners — Start Your Food Forest

Everything on this site works at backyard scale — the design logic in [Design: The Layers & Guilds](03-design-layers-and-guilds.md) doesn't change based on how much land you have. What changes is how you apply it: fewer canopy trees, tighter guild spacing, and a stronger weighting toward the layers that produce fast and don't need much room.

## Planning at backyard scale

Start with the same process as any scale — [Planning Your Site](02-planning-your-site.md) applies exactly as written, just over a smaller area. A few things matter more at this scale specifically:

- **Zone 1 does more work.** In a small yard, most of what you plant is close enough to count as zone 1 — visited daily, harvested often. Lean into that: put the plants you'll actually use in the kitchen regularly closest to your door.
- **Canopy count matters.** A full-size canopy tree can dominate a small lot's light and root space. One or two well-chosen canopy trees, with the rest of the layers filling in around them, usually works better than trying to fit several.
- **Vertical space is your friend.** The vine layer — passionfruit trained up a trunk or a fence line — lets you add a producing layer without using more ground footprint.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Skipping the observation season.** It's tempting to plant the week you get excited about the idea. Watching your actual sun, water, and wind patterns first (see [Planning Your Site](02-planning-your-site.md)) prevents the single most common failure: a planting placed somewhere the site never actually supported.
- **Planting canopy trees too close together.** They look small as saplings. Space them for their mature size, not their current size, or you'll be removing trees in five years that you spent five years growing.
- **Ignoring water until it's a problem.** Mulch and catchment planning (see [Soil, Water & Establishment](05-soil-water-and-establishment.md)) are cheap and easy before planting, expensive and disruptive to retrofit after.
- **Expecting garden-speed results.** A food forest's canopy takes years to produce (see [Timeline & What to Expect](06-timeline-and-what-to-expect.md)). Going in with that expectation set correctly is what keeps a quiet second year from feeling like a failed project.
- **Treating it as zero-maintenance.** It's differently maintained, not maintenance-free — see [Maintenance & Harvest](07-maintenance-and-harvest.md) for what the real, ongoing work actually looks like once established.
